## Draft narration (one continuous paragraph)
Put a ring around Earth like Saturn's, and a kid opening the curtains would see a stripe across the sky. It couldn't be ice, because ice wouldn't last this close to the Sun, so say it's rock. Where you stand decides what you see. At the equator, the ring is a thin line straight overhead. Farther north, it stretches into a wide arch. Far enough north, it never rises above the horizon. But Earth might not need a what-if. A study suggests that 466 million years ago, an asteroid passed too close, and Earth's pull tore it apart into a ring. The clue is in the craters: twenty-one from that era all formed near the equator. You see, a ring doesn't last forever. Its pieces slowly fall back, and near the equator is where they'd land. Some experts doubt the crater ages, but if the study is right, Earth once wore a ring, long before the dinosaurs. A what-if that may have been real.
