# EP003 — Concept card v1 (DRAFT, awaiting creator selection and approval)

**Pillar:** Weird Technology
**Creator seed (verbatim, 2026-11-02):**
> The Computer That Ran on Water
> Hook: "This computer didn't need a keyboard. It needed WATER."
> Visual: An illustrated engineer pours colored water into an enormous machine. The camera follows water through valves and tanks, revealing how the machine modeled an economy.
> Why it works: A completely unexpected combination of two familiar things. The MONIAC was a real hydraulic analog economic computer built in 1949.

## What the sources support (details in the research path)
- The MONIAC (Phillips Machine) was built by Bill Phillips and first shown at the LSE in 1949. It used coloured water to stand for money moving between tanks that stood for parts of the economy. [Wikipedia, "Phillips Machine"; Cambridge Faculty of Economics] (accessed 2026-11-02)
- Pumps, floats and drains set taxes, saving and spending; changing a tax rate meant changing a pumping speed. [Wikipedia, "Phillips Machine"]
- Phillips and Walter Newlyn reportedly tried other liquids (treacle, methylated spirits) before choosing coloured water. [Mental Floss, "Modeling Economics Using Water"; Wikipedia] — verify.
- About 12–14 machines were built; working ones survive (for example at Cambridge and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Museum). [Wikipedia; Smithsonian Magazine]
- **Disputed, do not state as fact:** built in a landlady's (or landlord's) garage in Croydon; scavenged Lancaster-bomber parts; "calibrated to 2% accuracy".

## Three framings (creator picks one, or a mix)

### A. "Watch the economy flow" (mechanism spine)
- **Central question:** How can a tank of water show how an economy works?
- **Hook promise:** an odd machine that looks like plumbing, then a clear "money is water" mechanism.
- **Audience assumption:** curious general viewer; no economics needed.
- **Emotional shape:** amusement → "oh, that's clever" (pour = income, pump = tax, drain = saving) → surprise (turn one dial and the whole system shifts) → reflective close.
- **Visual opportunity:** the camera rides one drop of coloured water through tanks, valves and pumps; a tax pump visibly speeds up; levels rise and fall like a fish tank.
- **Payoff:** what changing one setting did to the whole machine, shown instead of told.
- **Research path:** Phillips's own 1950 paper and the Science Museum and Cambridge records; confirm how a policy change was actually demonstrated.
- **Risks:** economics jargon; keep to three ideas (tank, pump, drain). Water must stay readable in 3D without a fluid simulation.

### B. "The engineer who built the economy" (human spine)
- **Central question:** Why would an engineer build an economy out of tanks?
- **Hook promise:** a person-and-problem story, then the machine as the answer.
- **Emotional shape:** puzzlement → scrappy invention → vindication (economists could finally *see* it).
- **Visual opportunity:** the engineer at work among scrap parts, then an audience of economists watching tanks fill.
- **Payoff:** the machine as proof that a picture can win a room.
- **Research path:** a biography of Bill Phillips and primary accounts of the first demonstration.
- **Risks:** more orientation up front; the most colourful details (garage, Lancaster parts) are disputed, so the story rests on thinner evidence. A real person is the lead character, so dramatized scenes must be labeled illustrative and avoid inventing his feelings.

### C. "What the water got wrong" (limits spine)
- **Central question:** If a water machine could model an economy, why don't we still use one?
- **Hook promise:** a quirky success, then an honest limit.
- **Emotional shape:** delight → "wait, it worked?" → deflation (it showed one flow well and left a lot out) → thoughtful close.
- **Visual opportunity:** the machine runs smoothly, then the camera pulls back to show everything it leaves out of frame.
- **Payoff:** a teaching tool, not a crystal ball.
- **Research path:** how economists judged the machine; the link between its shortcomings and Phillips's later work (reported, not confirmed).
- **Risks:** my searches found only one critic's view, so the evidence for "what it got wrong" is thin and needs expert sources before scripting.

## Mentor notes (critique, not approval)
- **Hook:** the seed line states the twist and compares to a keyboard, which most 1949 computers didn't have either. An anti-hook should be flat and concrete, for example (illustrative, creator may change): "In 1949, a London university switched on a machine that was mostly tanks of coloured water." Let the economy reveal come later.
- **One loop only:** pick one money loop (income → spending → tax back) and explain it once. Everything else is cut.
- **Honest wording:** "computer" means analog here; avoid "first" and "fooled" claims and the disputed details above.
- **Ending:** the seed has no consequence beat yet. Framings A–C each supply one; the creator chooses.
- **Tone:** dry humor works (economists watching a fish tank); the creator decides how jokey.
- **Visual note (v2 style):** day-lit lecture hall, then a darker demonstration with glowing tanks gives two lighting states. Water is faked with rising level planes and flowing texture rather than a fluid simulation.
