# Narration craft notes: the "story-chain" script format

**Source:** one creator-supplied narration script, titled "The Fake Town That Became Real" (about a mapmakers' trap that came true). The channel and video ID were not supplied. Analyzed 2026-11-02. Craft only: do not reuse the topic, sentences or wording. The story's facts were **not** checked here; any use of the story itself needs a claim ledger.

**Evidence limits:** one script, text only, no audio, no view or retention data. Structure and counts below are measured from the text; why it works is a hypothesis.

## Measured
| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Length | 159 words, 13 sentences (about 55–65 s at 2.5–2.7 words per second) |
| Average sentence | about 12 words; range 3–25 |
| Longest sentences | the setup (25 words) and the reveal (21 words) |
| Shortest sentences | the trigger (6), the accusation (5), the reversal (3), the closing callback (6) |
| Recurring anchor | the fake town's name appears 6 times; the title's key words return in the last line |
| Form | one continuous paragraph, no labels, no list-like fragments |

## Structure (what each sentence does)
| Sentences | Beat | Position | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook: a premise with a purpose** | 0–10% | An odd action stated with its reason ("they did X, just to catch Y"). It is concrete and complete, but it withholds the outcome. The title supplies the second half of the promise. |
| 2 | **One-line context** | 10–20% | One sentence on why the trap was possible ("back then, ..."). It answers the one question the hook raises and nothing more. |
| 3–4 | **Setup and rule** | 20–45% | The concrete details that make it real (when, where, what the thing was called) and the rule of the trap as "if X, then they'd know Y". |
| 5 | **Trigger** | 45% | The shortest turn in the setup: the trap fires. Six words. |
| 6–8 | **Escalation** | 45–65% | Proof in repeated detail ("exact same spot, exact same way"), then anger, then a threat, then one direct accusation. |
| 9–11 | **Reversal and proof** | 65–83% | A "but" flips the accusation; the three-word claim is followed by concrete evidence. |
| 12 | **Reveal** | 83–97% | A plain "you see" marks the explanation: the cause was the trap itself. It is the longest sentence after the setup. |
| 13 | **Closing callback** | last 4% | A six-word fragment that echoes the title and reframes the opening. |

## Hook, payoff and the link between them
- **The hook is a contrast pair.** The opening says "fake"; the title says "became real". The viewer waits for the moment those two meet.
- **The payoff is irony, delivered late.** The reveal that the trap created what it was meant to detect arrives in the last 17% of the words, and the final fragment states it in the title's own terms.
- **No sentence explains before it is needed.** The story names the cause only after the proof, so the reveal answers a question the viewer is already holding.
- **The title does half the hook's work,** so the narration does not restate it, and the opening line never previews the twist.

## Continuous storytelling: what keeps it moving
1. **Every sentence picks up the loose end of the one before.** Connectors do the work: "back then", "so", "if", "a few years later", "but", "and they had proof", "you see". There is no gap where the viewer has to guess the link.
2. **Time markers anchor the sequence** ("in the 1930s", "a few years later"), so cause and effect are always in order.
3. **One object, one running name.** The invented town is named once and then reused, never replaced by a synonym.
4. **Cast stays small and shifts by role:** the mapmakers, the other company, the people who opened the store. Each group appears when it matters and leaves.
5. **One short exchange at the peak.** The accusation and the denial are quoted lines. In this channel, dramatized dialogue must be **labeled illustrative** (see `me.md`) and never invented for real people without that label.
6. **Short sentences sit at the turns; long ones carry detail.** The trigger, accusation, reversal and callback are the shortest lines in the script.
7. **No meta-narration.** It never says "here's the setup", "but here's the twist" or asks the viewer a question. The chain itself carries the pivots.
8. **No filler or recap.** No sentence repeats information, and nothing follows the final callback.

## How this differs from our earlier scripts (EP002, EP003 v1)
| Aspect | EP002 / EP003 v1 | Story-chain format |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Numbered one-sentence lines with beat labels | One continuous paragraph |
| Connectors | Mostly fragments and signposts ("Here's the setup", "Now the strange part") | Plain connectors that carry cause and effect |
| Hook | Concrete scene, withheld explanation | Concrete premise with a stated purpose, withheld outcome |
| Pivot | Early explicit pivot line | The connectors do the pivoting; the reversal comes at about two thirds |
| Ending | Hedged or open | A reveal, then a short callback to the title |
| Actors | A character, a hypothetical, or none | A small cast of people with opposing goals |

## Use in this channel
- Keep the standing anti-hook preference by withholding the **outcome**, not the context: say what was done and why, and leave what happened for later.
- Write the narration as one continuous paragraph, then split it into beats for the storyboard.
- Build a cause-and-effect chain where each sentence answers the last and opens the next. Put the shortest sentences at the turns.
- Aim for one reversal and one reveal that explains it, then a callback to the title in a short last line.
- For topics with no people in conflict (a what-if, a mechanism), create the chain from the object's own causes and consequences, or a small cast of roles. Do not invent people's feelings or quotes.
- Hedge uncertain claims in the wording; this format must never trade accuracy for a neat twist.

## Boundaries
- Do not reuse the story, its sentences, or its structure as a signature. The channel voice stays in `channel-identity.md`.
- Do not claim this format causes views or retention.
