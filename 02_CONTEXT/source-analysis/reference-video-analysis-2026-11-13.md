## What the references do that our episodes do not
1. **They start at full brightness with words on screen.** Ours spend the first half second on a black fade-in.
2. **They say the premise and the stakes inside 3 seconds,** in 9–10 words. EP003 does this in its first sentence but takes 19 words. EP004 holds its what-if until 19 s, which the creator already flagged in `03_TRACKING/learnings.md`.
3. **They use short sentences and fragments.** "No broken bones. No poison. No head injuries." Their pace in words per minute is slower than ours, yet it reads faster because each sentence is one small step.
4. **Their captions are phrases, about twice our size, with one yellow keyword.** Ours show a single small white word.
5. **They cut to a new picture every 2–3 seconds.** Our camera glides through long continuous moves. We move more per frame than they do, so motion is not the gap; fresh pictures are. (This contradicts the "near-zero hard cuts" claim in `animation-style-notes-3d-cartoon-reference.md`, at least for these four videos.)
6. **Every number becomes an object on screen** on the word: a date card, "130 MILLION", a gauge, a clock, a battery icon. EP004 shows nothing but captions.
7. **A character carries the story.** It is in most shots and its face changes with the line. R3 and R4 make the viewer the character ("you"). R4 also shows viewer comments on screen and answers them ("Easy, I'll just get a generator." "Great, it runs for a few hours.").
8. **Something is always sounding.** Their audio never drops to silence; our preview files are narration over silence.
9. **They end on a full picture** with a loop or a punchline. Ours fade to black, which marks the end and gives no reason to rewatch.
10. **They are longer** (63–151 s vs our 36–53 s) and joke often.

## What we already share
- The same family of look: 3D sets with flat cartoon shading and ink outlines, egg-headed characters with stick limbs.
- A what-if premise (R2 and R4 are what-ifs).
- EP003's colour is close to R2 and R4 (saturated, dark). EP004 is paler and brighter than every reference.

## Improvements to consider (hypotheses; the creator decides)
**A. Finishing only: no Blender render needed**
1. Captions as 2–4 word phrases, about twice the size, one highlighted keyword.
2. No fade-in: first frame lit with the first caption already up.
3. No fade-out: end on a full picture.
4. A music bed and sound effects under the narration.

**B. Script**
1. Premise and stakes in the first sentence, 10 words or fewer.
2. Sentences of about 6–9 words; fragments allowed.
3. One idea per Short; the turn near the middle.
4. Second person where it fits.
5. A last line that loops into the first, or a callback joke.
6. A few light jokes.

**C. Picture**
1. A new picture every 2–3 seconds, with hard cuts allowed.
2. One on-screen object for each spoken number or key noun.
3. The character in most shots, with several faces per scene.
4. Stronger colour: saturation near EP003's, not EP004's.
5. Decide whether characters stay flat cards or become simple 3D figures like the references.

**D. Length**
1. Test a 60–75 s episode.

## The "invisible box" around our characters: found
The creator reported a box-like glitch around characters, worst when the camera zooms. It is real and visible in EP004's frames (S05 V2 and S07 V4 show it clearly).

- **Cause:** each character is a flat rectangular card with a transparent picture on it. The ink-line pass (Freestyle) treats the whole rectangle as solid when it works out which lines are hidden. So the outlines of everything behind the card vanish inside the card's rectangle: a hill's outline stops dead beside the kid and restarts on the other side. As the camera moves, lines pop in and out along the rectangle's edges.
- **Three related faults of the card approach:**
  - the card turns to face the camera every frame, so the feet slide on the ground during camera moves
  - the ground shadow is a fixed painted blob, not a real shadow
  - the card stays flat and evenly lit whatever the scene's light is
- **Fix:** build characters as real 3D objects, as both reference channels do. This removes the cause instead of patching it. (A patch exists for cards: cut each card's mesh to the character's outline, or render characters in a separate pass.)

## How the references build characters
Both channels use the same recipe, already described in `animation-style-notes-3d-cartoon-reference.md` and confirmed here by frames:
- **Head:** a sphere or egg, large (about a third of the height). Jaywalk tints it a skin tone; Afterhour Theory keeps it white.
- **Face:** flat painted eyes, brows and mouth on the front of the head, swapped per expression. No lip-sync.
- **Hair or hat:** a simple cap shape on the head.
- **Body:** a bean or capsule in one flat colour, with two or three details (buttons, a collar).
- **Limbs:** thin dark tubes with ball hands; simple shoes or none.
- **Acting:** the pose and the face change on the line; the body holds still between changes.

Because they are 3D, they take the scene's light and colour, cast real shadows, get the same outline as the set, and stay put when the camera orbits or zooms.

## Revised improvements, in order of likely effect (hypotheses)
1. **Topic and first line** matter most, by a wide margin, on this evidence. A subject the viewer already cares about (a famous name, a big release, "you"), with the stakes in the first sentence.
2. **3D characters** in the references' recipe: fixes the box and the sliding, and lets the character act in most shots.
3. **A colour and shading reset:** flat fills, two tones, darker and more saturated, clean gradient skies.
4. **Part 1's list still stands** for captions, cuts, on-screen numbers, no fades and length.
