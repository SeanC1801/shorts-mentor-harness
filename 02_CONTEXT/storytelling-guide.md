# Storytelling guide

**Status (2026-11-13):** the anti-hook and the story-chain format below remain the default. Ricks Clips, the most-viewed reference channel, uses this family of opening (a concrete contradiction) and this chain. Several changes are **under test**, and an episode whose concept card carries one applies it in place of the matching rule here (`03_TRACKING/format-tests.md`):
- a stakes-first opening: premise and stakes in the first sentence, about ten words (row 1)
- a title and first line that say the same thing (row 2)
- short sentences of about 6 to 9 words, fragments allowed (row 3)
- second person, "you" (row 4)
- an ending that loops into the first line, or a callback joke (row 5)
- a length of 60 to 90 seconds (row 13)

**Settled on 2026-11-13:** the title and first line are written in the topic stage of `/short-ideate`, before research; and from the 2026-11-11 learning, the premise goes in the first sentence and each Short carries one main idea.

1. Open with an **anti-hook** (creator's standing preference): a flat, understated, concrete line that withholds the explanation and raises a question. It must be honest and give just enough context to picture the scene.
2. Layer context gradually, one new piece per beat, so curiosity stays open until the story is complete. Orient early enough that the viewer is never lost; if a beat is confusing, add context before adding mystery.
3. Escalate with evidence, contrast, or a surprising mechanism.
4. Use a visual explanation or emotional turn, not narration alone.
5. Pay off the opening promise; end cleanly, optionally with a relevant open question.

## Script format: the story chain (creator's chosen format, 2026-11-02)
Write the narration as **one continuous paragraph** in which every sentence answers the one before it and opens the next. Analysis and measurements: `source-analysis/narration-style-notes-story-chain.md`.
1. **Hook = a premise with a purpose.** Say what was done and why, in one concrete sentence. Withhold the outcome, not the context. The title carries the second half of the promise; do not restate it.
2. **One sentence of context** that answers the single question the hook raises.
3. **Setup and rule:** concrete details (when, where, names) and the "if X, then Y" logic of the situation.
4. **Trigger:** the shortest line in the setup, where the situation changes.
5. **Escalation** through repeated concrete detail, conflict, or consequence.
6. **One reversal** (a plain "but") at about two thirds of the way, backed by concrete proof.
7. **Reveal:** a plain connective ("you see") and the explanation, placed in the last 15–20% of the words so it answers a question the viewer already holds.
8. **Closing callback:** a short last line that echoes the title and reframes the opening. Nothing after it.
Connectors carry the pivots ("back then", "so", "if", "a few years later", "but", "and they had proof"). Use time markers. Reuse one running name for the central thing. Keep the cast small. Put the shortest sentences at the turns. No meta-lines ("here's the twist"), no recap, no questions to the viewer.

**Story-chain check (before presenting a script):** (a) Does each sentence start from the loose end of the last? (b) Is the outcome withheld in line 1 and revealed late? (c) Is there exactly one reversal and one reveal? (d) Do the title's key words return in the last line? (e) Is any dramatized line labeled illustrative? (f) Does every factual claim appear in the ledger, with uncertain ones hedged in the wording? For topics with no people in conflict (a what-if, a mechanism), build the chain from the thing's own causes and consequences, and do not invent feelings or quotes.

Narration craft (attributed in `source-analysis/narration-style-notes.md`, `narration-style-notes-ricks-clips.md` and `narration-style-notes-story-chain.md`): short plain sentences, one idea each, one pivot into the explanation, cause-effect chain, end on the consequence, hedge uncertain claims.

Anti-hook check: Does line 1 create a specific question? Is there one clear image? Does each later beat answer something and open or deepen something? Is the promise honest and paid off? Whether anti-hooks outperform other hooks is unverified; treat as the channel's chosen style, not a proven rule.

Retention critique looks for confusion, repeated information, delayed context, flat emotional beats, and visuals that merely decorate speech. Do not treat platform metrics folklore as fact. The mentor proposes edits; creator selects.
